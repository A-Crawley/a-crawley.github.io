# World

## Logline

A village gets a Speaker, a stick for counting and a doctrine of Yield. One person keeps the count
while the village grows into something that no longer needs her to.

## Tone statement

Dry, specific, deadpan. The village's authority is a faith in productivity, and it speaks earnestly
in its own sacred jargon: blessings that read as targets, doctrine that reads as a quarterly plan.
The absurdity sits in the content. Narrators never wink. Funny first, then unsettling, with the
voice kept flat while the content darkens. Villagers stay sympathetic, and so does the Speaker. The
satire aims at metrics and growth logic wearing robes, never at a villager's dignity and never at
a real religion. Every rite, title and saying is invented. Do not borrow vocabulary, scripture,
calendar or ritual from real faiths.

## World rules

What the sim can and cannot do (story-facing, not for explanation in the text):

- The village runs on a clock the villagers cannot see. Days are called "Turns" and are numbered by
  the Speaker, who is told the number, not by anyone who counted.
- Things can appear without a carrier: food, bark ruled in columns, new arrivals. Nobody in the
  village treats this as strange, because the doctrine of Bearers and Arrivals covers it.
- Nobody has a past older than the first day. Asked, they answer in the doctrine's terms
  ("I was Received" and, later, "I was Onboarded").
- The sky repeats itself. Clouds are reused. The weather is always adequate.
- The sim cannot do real interiority for the villagers beyond what they say and do. Villagers are
  sympathetic in practice, not in explanation.

What villagers do not know: that they are in a simulation, that "the Spirit" is a single presence
who sometimes presses something, that the sky carries a counter, that there will be a rival.

### How the wisdom arrives

The Speaker, Dunstan Fallow, receives "the Word" at the lip of sleep and in the quiet before dawn.
The Word is what the Spirit says. It reaches him, and only him, as a voice like a ledger being
closed. He speaks it aloud at the **Stump of Saying**, where the paths cross, at dawn and at dusk,
and at any other time the Word arrives. Each pronouncement is a numbered **Saying**. The villagers
hear instructions, targets and reviews as sacred pronouncements. Nothing is written on a board.
The only things that appear in writing are the ruled bark the Speaker wakes holding, which he
cannot account for and does not try to.

The Word is the sim's output, and so is as dull and as exact as an output. Dunstan dresses it. He
adds a reason, a blessing and a figure of speech to whatever arrives, and what arrives is often
only a number. This is the gap the satire lives in, and it is also his private fear.

### The doctrine (invented, small, consistent)

- **The Spirit** gives. It gives "through hands": every Offering must have a **Bearer**, for the
  Spirit does not give to the empty-handed. This is the Source rule in sacred register.
- **The Yield** is what the village brings in. It is the thing worshipped. The greeting is "Eyes to
  the Yield." The reply is "And the Yield to us."
- **The Tally** is the count, and the **Keeper of the Tally** keeps it. An Offering with no named
  Bearer is **Orphaned**, and Orphaned Offerings are **Spoken Over** at the next **Hearing**. A
  Hearing is a review. Nobody has seen one end well.
- **Increase** is any amount over the Tally. The Spirit calls a spare notch Increase and is pleased.
- **Portion by Yield**: rations follow output, as doctrine ("the Spirit feeds those who are fed").
- **The Margin** is the sky. The Margin is "where the Spirit keeps what is not yet ours". It is not
  forbidden. It is **unlicensed**: to look into the Margin without cause is "reading above one's
  grade", a small impropriety, like singing at a Hearing.
- **The Latch** is the lever at the base of the Stump, burnt with LOOK UP and "Not yet ready."
  Doctrine: the Latch opens when the Yield is sufficient, at which point the Margin will be read
  aloud to everyone, by the Speaker, in order. Cormac says it is a hinge. Dunstan says that all
  latches are hinges that have been told something.
- Titles are the corporate noun phrase made holy: "blessed with the office of Records and
  Reconciliation".

How the drift shows up in daily life (never as a number, never named in the text):

- Efficiency side: Hearings, ranking read aloud, "Spoken Over", rounding down for the weak, rest
  renamed "Unyielding Hours", crediting someone with a Bearing they did not do so the Tally closes.
- Compassion side: rounding up, topping up a Portion, a "Quiet Column" nobody reads, rest.
- The drift is always seen as a small act by a named person. The Speaker's Sayings answer it
  afterwards in approving language, with a figure.

## Recurring gag and objects

- **The Stump of Saying.** Where the paths cross, if they are paths. Sayings are delivered from it.
  The Latch is set in its base.
- **The tally stick** (Marit's, story 1). A spare notch: the pit always holds one more than she has
  counted. The Spirit calls it Increase. Returns in stories 2 and 3.
- **"By my count."** Marit says it only before figures she is sure of. Later narrators may inherit
  it as a village idiom, losing the meaning.
- **The LOOK UP lever**, "Not yet ready." The village says it is a hinge. The Speaker says it is a
  Latch. Nobody says what it is for.
- **"Hands are invited to ..."** how the Word gives orders. (Hands: the village's term for workers.)
- **Warm things with no carrier.** Food that is warm when it should not be.
- **The ruled bark.** A strip of bark ruled in columns, found in Dunstan's hand. A form nobody drew.
- **Dunstan's hand going to his ear** whenever he says "the Spirit". He does not know he does it.
