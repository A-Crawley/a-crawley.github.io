The pit is four feet deep, lined with split bark, and holds nothing. I have counted it twice. By my count, the village has no food.

It is a good day for counting. A zero has no disputes in it.

The Speaker is already on the Stump when I climb out. Nobody saw him arrive. Cormac says he would have heard the climbing, and Cormac says this about most things he did not hear. Dunstan Fallow stands where the two paths cross, although the paths are not yet paths, in a cloak of sacking, with his eyes shut and his head tilted like a man listening at a wall. His hand goes to his ear and stays there.

"Hear this," he says, "and hear it as a figure."

We hear it.

> **SAYING 1.** The village is established. Our Yield is food. Let the Hands be turned toward it, and let them be turned in the direction of the food. Marit Dunnock, Keeper of the Tally, blessed with the office of Records and Reconciliation: may your numbers be honest, or at least agreed. So it is tallied.

I am M. Dunnock. I learn this at breakfast, from the Speaker, because there is no breakfast. I am the one of seven who can count past a hundred without using her feet, and the Spirit appears to have heard. He gives me a stick. It is ash, two feet long, with one face stripped for notches. He says he woke holding it, the way another man wakes holding a cramp. No instructions come with it, which I count as a good sign. Nobody has had time to get them wrong.

"Eyes to the Yield," says Dunstan, and opens his eyes.

No one has been taught the answer. The pause grows long enough to need one, and Pip, who is nine and has been lying in the dust, says, "And the Yield to us," and the Speaker looks at him as though he had been handed something back.

Wilf Ambrose is blessed Gatherer of the First Rank. His sack is blessed as well, to come back a third heavier than it goes. He nods at this the way you nod at a dog that has bitten somebody else. Then he takes the sack.

"Where will you go?" I ask.

"Where the food is."

"Where is that?"

"I'll know when I'm standing in it." He goes up the east slope, which is the only slope.

The Speaker steps down from the Stump and sits on its edge, a man who has carried something heavy a short way. I sit beside the pit with my stick. Hester Boyle, Warden of Timber, is at the edge of the trees explaining something to one of them. Cormac Tull, Raiser of Walls, is building the third hut, or has built two walls of it and is standing in the middle to see how it feels. Nan Prewitt sits in the doorway of the first hut with her hands in her lap, which the Saying does not mention. Pip counts ants. I have checked his total against mine and we do not agree about the ants.

At noon I count the pit again. Zero. I cut no notch. The stick and I are in agreement.

---

Wilf comes back at dusk. The sack hangs from his hand like a coat from a hook.

"How was it?" I say.

"A lot of walking."

"I need a number."

He holds out the sack. I look in it and record what I see, which is the inside of a sack. I have no notch for that. I put it in my head under _in: nothing_, and Wilf sits down on the pit lid, and Pip, whose ear is against the bark side of it, says, "There's one in there."

"There isn't," I say. "By my count."

"The wall's warm."

Wilf stands. I lift the lid.

It is a flat brown round, about the size of a palm, and it is warm in the way a loaf is warm, which I record separately, because a warm thing has come from somewhere. I have been beside this pit since noon. I looked away once, for the length of one sneeze, to pick up the stick where it had rolled. The lid was the same when I looked back. The pit was not.

"Mine?" says Hester, who has come over for the occasion.

"You were with a tree," says Cormac.

"It was a long conversation."

"It wasn't me," says Wilf. He opens the sack wider, in case we have missed something. We have not.

The Speaker has come over too. He does not ask who. Nobody asks Dunstan questions that begin with who. He holds out a strip of bark ruled in columns, the lines straight and dark, and says that he woke with it in his hand again. It has a heading, and he reads it as he reads everything, to the air a little above our heads.

"Tally of Offerings. Quantity. Bearer." His voice drops. "The Spirit gives through hands. It does not give to the empty-handed. An Offering without a Bearer is Orphaned, and Orphaned Offerings are Spoken Over at the next Hearing."

"When is that?" I say.

"It will be announced."

I read _Spoken Over_ twice.

I have a stick, a strip of bark and a loaf with no mother. I could write _none_. I try the shape of it in my head. A blank Bearer is a hole in the Tally, and the Spirit, which has gone to the trouble of ruling a column, clearly means it filled. If I leave the line open somebody else will put a name in it, and I would rather choose. There is one person in this village whose office is to bring food in. If the line is blank, it is his office that is blank.

I write _1_. Under Bearer I write _W. Ambrose_.

He reads it over my shoulder. He reads slowly, and I let him.

"I didn't," he says.

"It's a column."

"You said it to Pip." He means _by my count_. I say it ahead of every figure I am sure of and nothing ahead of the ones I am not, and I had not known anyone was listening for the difference. "You didn't say it to me."

"No."

He looks at his name for some time. Then he folds the sack and puts it under his arm, as though it might be needed.

Dunstan has not moved. "The Bearer is named," he says, to nobody. "It is a comfort to the Spirit when it is named." His hand is at his ear. I cut the first notch, and when I look up he has stopped speaking to us and is speaking to the air a little to the left. I did not hear it begin. Nobody did.

"Hear this, and hear it as a figure. One. The Spirit calls this a breaking-through and has appointed a Hearing. Let the Hands bring two. The Spirit does not ask for two. It leans toward it. So it is tallied."

This is Saying 2. I count it, because I count everything.

Pip is at the foot of the Stump, where there is a wooden lever set into the base, the length of a hand, with two words burnt into the wood beside it. LOOK UP. Under them, in smaller letters, a third line: _Not yet ready._ Cormac says it is a hinge.

"It is a Latch," says Dunstan, back in his own voice, low and tired. "All latches are hinges that have been told something."

Pip takes it in both hands and pulls, and it does not move, and he pulls again, and then he does what I have seen no one in the village do. He tips his head back and looks at the sky for a long time. The Speaker looks at his own feet, with the care of a man who has had practice.

"The Margin is not yet read to us, small one," he says. "It is a small impropriety, to read it early."

"Anything?" I say to Pip.

"I don't know yet."

I look up as well, to be fair to him. It is evening. There is cloud. There is nothing in it that I can count.

"By my count, nothing," I say, and hear that I have said it, and cannot tell which of us I am trying to convince.

The Spirit wants two. Wilf leaves at first light with a sack and a name on a strip of bark, and no idea where the loaf came from. Tomorrow I will go with him. Someone should count what he walks past.

## Echoes for the game

- The first-food log line could carry the story's version: a warm loaf in an empty store, and a Tally of Offerings that will not take a blank under Bearer.
- "An Offering without a Bearer is Orphaned, and Orphaned Offerings are Spoken Over at the next Hearing." "It will be announced." Fits a Forager flavour line or the first performance review.
- "All latches are hinges that have been told something." A direct cousin of the locked button; the unlock log could say someone has stopped calling it a hinge.
- "May your numbers be honest, or at least agreed." A records-related blessing, or an achievement description.
- "The Spirit does not ask for two. It leans toward it." A target as a gentle lean: a stage 1 goal line or a log line at the first quarterly review.
- A villager who says "By my count" only before the figures they are sure of: a tic the log could give to a recurring name.
